# Marketing Psychology Playbook

Compiled 2026-11-21. This is the reference the site audits are scored against.

**How it was built.** Web research across secondary sources (book summaries, trade press, research-institute pages, a few academic papers) on top of general knowledge of the books. No book was read in full: the modern titles are in copyright, and the public-domain ones (Hopkins, Scott, Bernays) could not be fetched because this session's network blocks archive.org, Gutenberg and Wikisource. Where a claim rests on a single weak source or on memory, it is marked.

**Evidence labels used below**

- **Strong** — large datasets, meta-analyses or multi-lab replications.
- **Practitioner** — decades of tested direct-response practice, but not controlled science.
- **Contested** — popular, but replications are mixed or the framework is disputed.

---

## 1. The one-page version

1. Desire already exists; marketing points it at the product. Match the message to how aware the buyer is.
2. Be known for one thing, and look like yourself everywhere.
3. Lead with the buyer's problem and outcome, not with the company.
4. Feeling builds the brand over years; reasons close the sale today. Do both, in different places.
5. Specific, checkable claims beat adjectives.
6. Perceived value is the outcome and its believability, divided by the wait and the effort.
7. Reduce the risk of saying yes: proof, guarantees, clear returns, no surprises at checkout.
8. Remove friction before adding persuasion.
9. Test. Most "laws" are averages, and several famous effects are weaker than the books claim.

## 2. Who is reading: awareness and market sophistication

**Source:** Eugene Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966). **Practitioner.**

Five levels of awareness, and what the page must do at each:

| Level | Buyer's state | Lead with |
|---|---|---|
| Unaware | Does not feel a problem | A story or identity they recognise |
| Problem-aware | Feels the problem, knows no solution | The problem, named precisely |
| Solution-aware | Knows solutions exist, not yours | Why this type of solution, then why you |
| Product-aware | Knows you, unsure it fits | Proof, comparisons, objections answered |
| Most-aware | Convinced | The offer: price, terms, a reason to act now |

Audit use: identify which level each traffic source delivers (Pinterest and social are mostly unaware or problem-aware; branded search is product-aware) and check the landing page matches it.

**Market sophistication** asks how many times the market has already heard the claim. In a new market a plain claim works. As competitors pile in, bigger claims stop working and the message has to move to *how* it works (the mechanism), and finally to identity and story. The stage-by-stage detail I found online was inconsistent between sources, so treat the direction as reliable and the stage numbering as unverified.

## 3. What we stand for: positioning

**Sources:** Ries & Trout, *Positioning* and *The 22 Immutable Laws of Marketing*; April Dunford, *Obviously Awesome*. **Practitioner.**

- A brand occupies one idea in the mind. Being first in a category beats being better in someone else's; if you can't be first, define a narrower category you can lead.
- Focus means sacrifice: fewer claims, a defined customer, a product line that fits the idea.
- Dunford's working components (the search results confirmed the framework's shape but not every name, so the list is partly from memory):
  1. **Competitive alternatives** — what the customer would do if you did not exist, including "nothing".
  2. **Unique attributes** — what you have that those alternatives lack.
  3. **Value** — what those attributes let the customer achieve.
  4. **Best-fit customers** — who cares most about that value.
  5. **Market category** — the frame that makes the value obvious.
  6. (Plus) **relevant trends** that make it timely.

Positioning statement to fill in per brand: *For [best-fit customer] who [situation], [brand] is the [category] that [value], unlike [alternative], because [proof].*

## 4. Being remembered: distinctiveness and mental availability

**Sources:** Byron Sharp, *How Brands Grow*; Jenni Romaniuk, *Building Distinctive Brand Assets* (Ehrenberg-Bass Institute). **Strong data, contested interpretation.**

- Brands grow mainly by reaching more buyers, most of them light and occasional, not by deepening loyalty among existing ones.
- The job is to come to mind in buying situations and be easy to buy. Distinctive assets (colour, logo, typeface, a phrase, a visual style) do that work.
- Romaniuk scores each asset on **fame** (how many category buyers link it to the brand) and **uniqueness** (whether it evokes only that brand). The institute uses 50% on both as the bar. High on both: protect and never change. High uniqueness, low fame: invest. High fame, low uniqueness: a category cliché, sharpen or drop.
- Critics (reported in WARC) say Sharp undervalues differentiation and that "availability" is loosely defined; one academic thesis found the approach suits market leaders best and applies to niche brands only within limits.

**Working position for small brands:** both are needed. Positioning decides who we are for and what we claim (section 3); distinctiveness makes sure the same recognisable brand delivers it every time. Do not redesign for novelty.

## 5. Emotion and reason: the long and the short

**Source:** Binet & Field, *The Long and the Short of It* (IPA Databank, 996 campaigns, 1980–2010). **Strong**, though drawn from award entries and mostly large brands.

- Average best split of budget: about 60% brand building, 40% sales activation. It varies by category (reported as high as 80/20 in financial services).
- Brand building works through emotion and broad reach and pays back slowly. Activation works through rational, specific offers to people already in the market and pays back fast, then fades.
- Trying to do both in one piece of creative usually weakens both.
- Later evidence reported by Mark Ritson: brand advertising also lifts short-term sales, while activation leaves little long-term effect.

Audit use: check each brand has both layers. A site that is all discount codes has no brand layer; one that is all mood has no reason to buy today.

## 6. The message: clarity, story, stickiness

**StoryBrand (Donald Miller). Practitioner.** Seven parts: a *character* (the customer) wants something, has a *problem*, meets a *guide* (the brand), who gives a *plan* and a *call to action*, leading to *success* and away from *failure*. The "grunt test": within a few seconds of landing, a visitor can say what you offer, how it improves their life, and what to do next. Sources disagree on whether that is 3 or 10 seconds.

**Made to Stick (Chip & Dan Heath). Practitioner.** Ideas stick when they are Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, and told as Stories.

**Contagious (Jonah Berger). Research-based, moderate.** Things get shared when they carry Social currency (sharing makes me look good), Triggers (everyday cues that bring it to mind), Emotion (high-arousal feelings such as awe), Public visibility, Practical value, and Stories. Most relevant to Pinterest and social content.

**Copy craft (Hopkins, Caples, Ogilvy). Practitioner; quotes reached me second-hand.**

- The headline carries most of the result; a long headline that says something beats a short one that says nothing.
- Specifics over generalities; numbers, names, processes.
- Long copy outsold short copy in their split tests, *when* it was well written and the reader was interested. Length should follow how much the buyer needs to know, not a rule.
- Hopkins's central rule: treat advertising as salesmanship and measure it. Test, keep the winner.

## 7. The offer: making value obvious

**Source:** Alex Hormozi, *$100M Offers*. **Practitioner.**

Perceived value rises with the **dream outcome** and the **perceived likelihood of getting it**, and falls with **time delay** and **effort and sacrifice**. Four levers, four audit questions:

- Is the outcome stated in the customer's terms?
- What on the page makes success believable (proof, guarantee, track record)?
- How fast is the first result, and do we say so?
- What work or hassle have we removed, and do we say so?

Building the offer: list every obstacle between the buyer and the outcome, turn each into a solution, bundle the high-value, low-cost ones, then add bonuses, a guarantee, and honest scarcity or urgency. Name it.

**Jobs to be done (Christensen; forces model by Moesta & Spiek). Practitioner.** People "hire" a product to make progress in a specific situation; the job has functional, emotional and social sides. A switch happens only when the *push* of the current frustration plus the *pull* of the new option outweigh *anxiety* about the new and *habit* with the old. Most pages work only on pull. Guarantees, trials and clear returns reduce anxiety; easy setup reduces habit.

**Reframing (Rory Sutherland, *Alchemy*). Practitioner, anecdotal.** People respond to what something means, not its specification. Changing the name, the frame of comparison or the signal can change value without changing the product. Visible effort and expense signal confidence. The Red Bull and "Chilean sea bass" examples are from summaries, not checked against the book.

## 8. Trust and persuasion

**Cialdini, *Influence* and *Pre-Suasion*. Mixed: the classic six are well supported; unity less so.**

| Principle | Honest use on a site |
|---|---|
| Reciprocity | Give something useful first: a guide, a tool, a sample |
| Commitment & consistency | Small first step (quiz, save, email) before the big ask |
| Social proof | Reviews, counts, customer photos near the decision point |
| Authority | Credentials, press, expertise shown not asserted |
| Liking | A human voice, real faces, shared taste |
| Scarcity | Only when real: limited stock, a genuine deadline |
| Unity | Shared identity: "people like us" language and community |

**Reviews (Spiegel Research Center with PowerReviews, 2015–17). Strong for its dataset, now dated.**

- Purchase likelihood peaks at an average rating of roughly 4.2–4.5 stars and falls toward a perfect 5.0, which shoppers read as too good to be true.
- A product with five reviews was 270% more likely to be bought than one with none; most of the gain comes in the first five to ten.
- Verified-buyer labels add a further lift (about 15% in one summary).

Implication: get the first five reviews on every product, show negative ones, and never curate to a perfect score.

**Services (Parasuraman, Zeithaml & Berry; Lovelock & Wirtz). Academic.** A service cannot be inspected before purchase, so buyers judge it on surrounding cues: the look of the site, the people, the process, the price. Price itself becomes a quality signal when little else is available. Buyers reduce risk by seeking recommendations, reputation, guarantees and a way to try first. A service page therefore needs to make the intangible visible: named people, a described process, case results, a guarantee, a low-risk first step.

## 9. Pricing and choice: what actually holds up

Several famous behavioural effects are weaker than the popular books suggest.

| Effect | Status | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Anchoring on factual estimates | **Strong** (multi-lab replication) | Show the reference price or the costlier option first |
| Anchoring on what people will pay | **Contested** (a direct replication of Ariely's study failed; weaker with real money at stake) | Use, but expect modest effects |
| Charm pricing (.99) | **Small but real** (2023 meta-analysis, 69 studies, g ≈ 0.13); one large online experiment found nothing | Fine for value lines; round prices suit premium |
| Choice overload | **Conditional** (appears when options are hard to compare and no option stands out) | Curate, give a recommended pick, make comparison easy |
| Decoy / compromise effect | **Unresolved** from what I found | Test three-tier pricing; do not assume it works |
| Behavioural priming (subtle cues changing behaviour) | **Weak** (large replications failed) | Ignore |

## 10. Friction

**Source:** Baymard Institute. **Strong** (large-scale usability testing; detailed rankings are paywalled).

- Average cart abandonment is about 70%. A large share is people just browsing, which design cannot fix.
- The fixable causes Baymard highlights: extra costs revealed late (shipping, tax), forced account creation, and long or confusing checkout forms. A 2017 summary claimed the average checkout has about twice the form fields it needs (via a sponsored post).
- 65% of sites benchmarked "mediocre" or worse on checkout.

Rule: before adding any persuasion device, remove a step, a field, a surprise or a doubt.

---

## 11. Where the books disagree

- **Differentiate or be distinctive?** Ries/Trout/Dunford versus Sharp. Resolution in section 4: small brands need a sharp position *and* consistent assets.
- **Target narrowly or reach broadly?** Positioning says narrow; Sharp says reach every category buyer. A small brand starts narrow because it cannot afford broad reach, and widens as it grows.
- **Emotion or reasons?** Both, separated by job (section 5).
- **Long or short copy?** As long as the buyer's remaining questions require.

---

## 12. Limits

No framing makes a purchase inevitable. These principles raise the odds when a product genuinely fits a desire; they do not create the fit. Invented scarcity, fake reviews or hidden costs produce one sale and lose the customer, and several are illegal in consumer-protection law. Every tactic here is to be used with true claims only.

## 13. Audit scorecard

Score each brand's site 0–2 per line (0 absent, 1 partial, 2 strong).

**Positioning and brand**
1. A first-time visitor can say what this is, who it is for and why it is different within five seconds.
2. One clear idea is owned; the site does not claim several unrelated things.
3. Distinctive assets (colour, type, imagery, phrases) are consistent across every page and channel.

**Message**
4. The hero section leads with the customer's problem or outcome, not the company.
5. Landing pages match the awareness level of their traffic source.
6. Claims are specific and checkable.
7. There is an emotional brand layer as well as rational reasons to buy.

**Offer**
8. The outcome, its believability, the speed and the ease are each stated.
9. Reference prices or comparisons frame the price.
10. There is a guarantee or other risk reversal, stated near the buy button.

**Trust**
11. Reviews or other proof appear at the point of decision; every product has at least five.
12. Real people, process and credentials are visible (critical for services).
13. Any scarcity or urgency is real.

**Friction**
14. One primary call to action per page, repeated.
15. Total cost, including shipping, is visible before checkout.
16. Guest checkout, minimal fields, fast on mobile.

**Growth loop**
17. There is a low-commitment first step (guide, quiz, sample, email) for visitors not ready to buy.
18. Something on the site is worth sharing.
