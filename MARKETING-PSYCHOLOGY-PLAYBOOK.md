# Marketing Base Principles

Version 2, 2026-11-21. Practitioner edition. This is the foundation every brand audit, page, ad, pin, video and email is built on and checked against.

## How this was built, and how far to trust it

- **Sources:** about 45 web searches across what the great practitioners taught (direct-response copywriters, agency founders, brand builders, modern operators), plus the platforms' own creative guidance for TikTok and Pinterest.
- **Not read in full:** no book was read cover to cover. The modern ones are in copyright; the public-domain ones were blocked by this session's network.
- **TikTok:** I cannot open the TikTok app or watch videos. The TikTok section is built from TikTok's own published creative guidance and from what TikTok marketers publish about what works. Vendor statistics are treated as direction, not fact.
- **The (M) tag:** a point marked (M) comes from my prior knowledge of that practitioner and was not re-confirmed by a source in this session. Everything unmarked was found in a source listed at the end.
- Everything here is a starting position to be tested on our own traffic, which is itself the first rule every one of these people taught.

# Part 1 — The twelve base laws

These are the points where the practitioners agree with each other across a hundred years. Everything later in the document is detail hanging off one of these.

1. **Market before message, message before medium.** Find people who already want something badly, then decide what to say, then choose where to say it (Halbert, Kennedy, Schwartz, Godin).
2. **You cannot create desire. You can only aim it.** Take the hopes and fears already in the buyer and point them at the product (Schwartz).
3. **Stand for one thing.** The most important decision is how the product is positioned; boil the strategy down to one promise and deliver it completely (Ogilvy, Ries & Trout).
4. **The offer does most of the work.** A strong offer with average copy beats brilliant copy on a weak offer (Halbert, Kennedy, Hormozi).
5. **Enter the conversation already going on in the buyer's head.** Start where their attention is, in their words, not ours (Collier (M), Wiebe).
6. **The headline or hook is most of the result.** If it fails, nothing after it is seen (Caples, Ogilvy, Hopkins, Brunson, every short-form video operator).
7. **Specific beats general. Always.** A claim you can picture and check outsells an adjective (Hopkins, Caples, Dry).
8. **Proof beats promise.** Buyers are sceptical by default; belief is the gate before the sale, so proof goes early and often (Bencivenga).
9. **Take the risk off the buyer.** Whoever carries the risk controls the sale; carry it for them (Abraham).
10. **Clarity beats cleverness and beats persuasion.** A confused visitor does nothing (Kennedy, Laja, Miller).
11. **Always tell them exactly what to do next, and why now.** Every piece has an offer, a reason to act today and clear instructions (Kennedy).
12. **Test everything and believe the numbers over the opinions.** Including ours, and including this document (Hopkins, Caples, Ogilvy, Laja).

And one rule above the twelve: **tell the truth.** Sugarman ranked honesty as the single most important trigger. Bernbach's agency was founded on it. A lie buys one sale and costs the customer.

# Part 2 — The market

**Halbert's starving crowd.** Offered any advantage in a hamburger-stand contest, he would take only one: a starving crowd. Pick the hungry market before perfecting the product. The way to know what people want is to look at what they already buy.

**Kennedy's triangle.** Right message, right market, right media. None works without the other two. If a channel cannot carry a direct-response message, drop it.

**Godin's smallest viable audience.** Choose the smallest group that can sustain the business and serve it so well it talks. Define the group by what it believes and wants, not by age and postcode. Find the lock, then cut the key. Belonging is the engine: people buy what people like them buy (M).

**Schwartz's awareness ladder.** Every page, ad and video is written for one rung.

| Rung | They know | Open with |
|---|---|---|
| Unaware | Nothing | A story, an identity, a striking picture |
| Problem-aware | The problem | The problem, named better than they could |
| Solution-aware | That solutions exist | Why this kind of solution, then why ours |
| Product-aware | Us | Proof, comparison, objections answered |
| Most-aware | They want it | The deal: price, terms, deadline |

**Schwartz's sophistication rule.** The more times a market has heard a claim, the less a bigger claim works. Move from the claim, to how it works (the mechanism), to who the buyer becomes by choosing it.

**Wiebe's research rule.** Do not write copy; collect it. Read reviews of our products and competitors', forum threads, support emails and survey answers, and lift the exact phrases customers use for the pain, the wish and the hesitation. Her most famous headline was taken word for word from a customer review.

**Christensen's job.** Ask what the buyer is hiring the product to do in a specific moment, and what they would have hired instead. The real competitor is often not another brand.

**Minute details**

- Keep a running "voice of customer" file per brand: pains, desired outcomes, objections, exact phrases. Every headline should be traceable to it.
- For each traffic source, write down the awareness rung it delivers. Social and Pinterest: unaware to problem-aware. Generic search: solution-aware. Branded search and email: product-aware to most-aware.
- One page, one reader, one rung, one action (M).

# Part 3 — Positioning and brand

**Ogilvy.** Positioning is the first and biggest decision. One large promise, delivered wholeheartedly. Every single advertisement is a contribution to the brand's long-term image, so nothing goes out that cheapens it. Do not try to be all things to all people. Make the product the hero.

**Ries & Trout.** Own one word or idea. Being first in the mind beats being better. If the category is taken, define a narrower one we can lead. Focus requires giving things up.

**Steve Jobs (1997).** The world is noisy and nobody will remember much about any company, so be clear about the one thing we want remembered. Marketing is about values, not specifications or comparisons. Nike sells shoes but never talks about soles; it honours athletes.

**Bernbach.** Start from a sound premise about the product before trying to be creative. Find the essence of the product. Modest, concrete, honest claims are more believable than loud ones ("Think Small" sold a small car on mileage and parking). Rules followed blindly produce mediocrity. A principle only counts when it costs you money.

**Dunford's working method.** Position against what the customer would otherwise do; name what only we have; translate it into the value it creates; pick the customers who care most; choose the category that makes the value obvious (partly M).

**Kapferer on premium and luxury.** The rules invert as the brand moves upmarket:

- Do not compare yourself to anyone; premium is not comparative.
- Do not chase rising demand; scarcity that is real protects desire.
- The brand sets the price; the price does not make the brand.
- Raise prices over time rather than discounting.
- These apply to genuine luxury. For accessible-premium brands, take the direction (never discount the core, never apologise for price, protect the look) without the extremes.

**Distinctive assets (Romaniuk).** Choose a small set of brand signals — a colour, a typeface, a photographic style, a phrase, a shape — and repeat them without variation for years. Boredom inside the company is not a reason to change them.

**Minute details**

- Write the positioning in one sentence: *For [who] who [situation], [brand] is the [category] that [single promise], because [proof].* If it needs "and", it is not finished.
- One promise per brand, one supporting promise per product.
- Name the enemy: the alternative, habit or frustration we are against. It sharpens everything.
- Same logo placement, same colours, same type, same photo treatment, same tone on site, email, pins, video covers and packaging.
- The About page is a positioning page: why we exist, what we believe, who it is for. Not a company history.

# Part 4 — The offer

**Hormozi's value equation.** Perceived value goes up with the dream outcome and the buyer's belief they will get it, and goes down with the time it takes and the effort it costs. Improve all four, and say so on the page.

**Building the offer (Hormozi).** State the dream outcome. List every obstacle between the buyer and it. Turn each obstacle into a solution. Bundle the solutions that are high value to them and low cost to us. Add bonuses, a guarantee, and real scarcity or urgency. Give it a name.

**Abraham's risk reversal.** Put the guarantee at the centre of the selling message, and make it stronger than the industry norm — he suggested two to three times. Few buyers claim; many more buy.

**Abraham's three ways to grow.** More customers, bigger average purchase, more frequent purchase. Small gains in each multiply. Every offer should be judged on which of the three it moves.

**Abraham's preeminence.** Treat buyers as clients under our care: recommend what is right for them, even when it is the cheaper option. Advice given that way is what makes people buy the expensive one.

**Kennedy's rules.** There is always an offer. There is always a reason to respond now. The instructions are always clear. Results are tracked. Follow-up never stops (last two M).

**Brunson's value ladder (M).** A free or cheap first step, then a core product, then a premium one. Each rung earns the trust to offer the next.

**Hormozi's lead magnet.** Give away something that fully solves one small, specific problem and, in solving it, reveals the bigger problem the paid product solves.

**Minute details**

- Every page states: what you get, what it costs, what happens next, what if you do not like it.
- Put the guarantee beside the buy button, not in the footer. Name it and give it a number of days.
- Urgency must be true: a real deadline, real stock, a real seasonal reason. State the reason.
- Bundles: let the buyer choose components where possible; a fixed bundle with no choice often fails.
- Show the price with an anchor: the separate-items total, the per-use cost, or the price of the alternative.
- Three options when there are tiers, with the one we want chosen marked as recommended. Test it; do not assume.
- Order bumps and post-purchase offers go after the main decision, never before it.

# Part 5 — Copy, line by line

## The structures

- **Schwab's five:** get attention; show an advantage; prove it; persuade them to grasp it; ask for action.
- **Brunson:** hook, story, offer. If something is not working it is always one of the three.
- **Problem, agitate, solve** (Wiebe's workhorse): name the problem, make its cost felt, present the answer.
- **StoryBrand:** customer as hero with a problem, brand as guide with a plan, a call to action, success shown, failure avoided.
- **Bencivenga's equation (M):** an urgent problem, a unique promise, unquestionable proof, an easy proposition.

## Headlines

- The headline's job is to select the right reader and make them read the next line.
- Lead with self-interest or news; curiosity alone is weak, curiosity plus benefit is strong (Caples, M).
- A long headline that says something beats a short one that says nothing (Caples).
- Length is not the variable; content is (Ogilvy's own tests found either can work).
- Harry Dry's three tests for any line: Can I picture it? Can it be proved false? Could no competitor say it? Three noes means rewrite.
- Put the product's name or category in the headline when the reader would otherwise not know what is being sold (M).
- Write twenty before choosing one (M).

## The opening

- Sugarman's slippery slide (M): the only job of the first sentence is to get the second one read. Keep it short. Remove every reason to stop.
- Open inside the reader's situation, in their words.
- Do not open with the company, its founding year or the word "welcome".

## The body

- Talk to one person. Use "you" far more than "we".
- Features become benefits by adding "so you can" or "which means".
- One idea per sentence. Short paragraphs. Subheads that make sense read alone, because most people scan.
- Give reasons. A claim with "because" is believed more than a bare claim (Hopkins, M).
- Tell the whole story for the reader who wants it; let the scanner get the gist from headings, captions and bullets. Long copy outsold short in Ogilvy's split tests when it was worth reading.
- Captions under images are read about twice as much as body copy (Ogilvy). Every image caption is a selling line.
- Admit a limitation. A small honest negative makes the positives credible (Bernbach, Sugarman).
- Cut every sentence that could appear on a competitor's site unchanged (Dry).
- Wiebe edits in separate passes — for clarity, for voice, for proof, for specificity, for emotion, for zero risk — one thing at a time.

## Bullets

- Each bullet is a small headline: a specific benefit plus a touch of curiosity (Bencivenga's speciality).
- Lead with the benefit, not the feature. Three to seven, not fifteen.
- Use numbers, materials, dimensions, times.

## Sugarman's triggers

His book lists about two dozen. Those confirmed in this session's sources: involvement and ownership (get the reader imagining it in their life), honesty, integrity, credibility, value and proof of value, justifying the purchase (give the logical excuse for the emotional decision), greed (a bargain), current fads, and the satisfaction conviction — a guarantee so strong it goes beyond the ordinary trial period and signals total confidence.

## The close and the call to action

- Summarise the promise, restate the guarantee, give the reason to act now, tell them exactly what to click and what happens after.
- Button text says what they get, not what they do: "Get the guide", not "Submit".
- One primary action per page, repeated after each major section.
- Reduce the fear at the button: "Free returns", "Takes two minutes", "No account needed".

# Part 6 — Proof

Bencivenga's rule: belief comes before desire. Put proof near the top, not at the bottom.

**Kinds of proof, strongest first (M for the ranking)**

1. Demonstration — show it working, before and after, in use.
2. Specific results with numbers.
3. Reviews and testimonials with full names, photos, details.
4. Third-party endorsement: press, experts, certifications, awards.
5. Volume: customers served, units sold, years trading.
6. The guarantee itself.
7. Transparency: how it is made, where, by whom, what it costs to make.

**Minute details**

- A testimonial should name the doubt the person had, then the result. "I wasn't sure about X, but…" is the most useful sentence a customer can write.
- Place the proof that answers an objection beside the place the objection arises: a sizing review next to the size selector, a delivery review next to the shipping line.
- Show the star rating near the title and full reviews lower down.
- Do not hide the bad reviews. A near-perfect but not perfect rating converts best.
- Get the first five reviews on every product before spending on traffic to it.
- Real photographs outperform illustrations for selling (Ogilvy). Real customers outperform models for trust.
- Never invent proof.

# Part 7 — Page anatomy

## The first screen (any page)

The visitor must be able to answer three questions without scrolling, within about five seconds: What is this? Is it for me? What do I do next?

- A headline that states the outcome or the offer.
- One supporting line that says who it is for and how.
- One primary button.
- One image that shows the product in use or the result.
- One line of proof (rating, count, press).
- Nothing that moves, slides or competes.

## Home page order

1. First screen as above.
2. The problem, in the customer's words.
3. The solution and the single promise.
4. How it works, in three steps (the plan).
5. Proof.
6. The products or services, each with one benefit line.
7. The guarantee.
8. The low-commitment offer for people not ready (guide, quiz, sample).
9. Final call to action.

## Product page order

1. Title that includes what it is. Rating beside it.
2. Images: in use, in scale, in detail, in context; then any video.
3. Price with its anchor; total cost including delivery visible or one tap away.
4. Three to five benefit bullets.
5. Options, quantity, buy button. Guarantee and delivery promise directly under the button.
6. The story: who it is for, the problem, how it solves it, how it is made.
7. Specifications in a table for the reader who needs them.
8. Objections as questions and answers.
9. Reviews.
10. What goes with it.
11. Buy button again.

## Service page order

1. Outcome headline, who it is for, primary action (book, call, quote).
2. The problem and what it is costing them.
3. The process, step by step, with what happens at each and how long it takes.
4. Who does the work: names, faces, credentials.
5. Results: cases with numbers.
6. Price, or at least a range and what drives it.
7. The guarantee or the low-risk first step.
8. Questions answered honestly, including who it is not for.
9. Primary action again.

## Friction details

- Remove every form field that is not needed today.
- Allow guest checkout.
- Never reveal a cost for the first time at checkout.
- Navigation: few items, plain labels. On a campaign landing page, remove the navigation.
- Speed: the page must feel instant on a phone on mobile data.
- Tap targets large enough for a thumb; the buy button reachable without stretching.
- Error messages say what to do, in plain words.
- Clarity beats persuasion: fix what is confusing before adding anything that sells (Laja).

# Part 8 — Short-form video and TikTok

## What the platform itself says

TikTok's creative guidance is built around six codes: make it for TikTok first, give it structure, add stimulation, use sound, use trends, and keep production native.

- **Structure:** hook, then body about the product, then a close with a call to action.
- **Hook window:** TikTok's own performance guidance says the first six seconds are vital. Practitioners work to a much tighter window of one and a half to three seconds. Design for the tight one.
- **Emotion in the hook:** suspense, surprise or another feeling, straight away.
- **Native beats polished:** content that looks like it belongs in the feed outperforms content that looks like an advert.
- **Sound and text:** design for sound on, but put the hook in on-screen text too, because many people start with sound off.

## Hormozi's content rule

Hook, retain, reward. Stop the scroll; hold attention; pay off the promise so fully that the viewer feels it was worth their time and shares it. A video that hooks and does not reward teaches the audience to stop trusting our hooks.

## The MrBeast production principles

His leaked internal guide treats three numbers as the whole job: click-through rate (title and cover), average view duration, and average view percentage. The first minute is where most viewers are lost, so it gets the most planning. Ideas should be both exciting and simple enough to grasp instantly. Take the timeless part — attention, pacing, clarity, payoff — and leave the stunt budgets.

## Hook patterns that marketers report working

- **Open loop:** start something the viewer must stay to see finished.
- **Pattern interrupt or contrarian:** contradict what they expect.
- **Identity call-out:** name the exact viewer ("If you have a small living room…").
- **Specific outcome:** from this to that, with a number.
- **Problem first:** negative framings are reported to hold better than positive ones.
- **Show the result first,** then how.

## Video structure for selling

Hook (0–2s) → proof or result (2–5s) → demonstration → offer → call to action.

## Minute details

- The first frame must work as a still image; it is also the cover.
- A person's face or hands in the first second.
- Movement in the first second: a cut, a reveal, an action already under way.
- On-screen hook text large, high on the frame, inside the safe area, readable in one glance.
- Captions throughout; emphasise the key words.
- Change something every two to three seconds: angle, zoom, text, b-roll.
- No logo sting, no intro, no "hi guys".
- One idea per video.
- End on the payoff and the next step; do not trail off.
- Vertical 9:16, filmed on a phone, natural light.
- Post the same idea with five different hooks before deciding the idea failed. Let the retention graph choose.
- Track three numbers per video: the share still watching at three seconds, average watch time, and completion rate. Then saves and shares before likes.
- The comment section is research: every question is the next video.

## What makes it spread (Berger)

It makes the sharer look good; it is tied to an everyday trigger; it stirs a strong feeling; it is visible; it is useful; it is wrapped in a story.

# Part 9 — Pinterest

From Pinterest's own creative guidance.

- Vertical, 2:3 (for example 1000 × 1500). Other shapes get cut off.
- The image must show why the product fits the viewer's life: in context, in use, in a real room.
- Short text overlay, readable on a phone. For video pins, text or captions carry the story because sound is usually off.
- A subtle logo on every pin, never in the lower-right corner, where the interface covers it.
- Board titles short, with the keywords people search. Descriptions with accurate keywords.
- Plan by season using Pinterest Trends; people search weeks ahead of the moment.
- Pinterest users are planning, not scrolling for entertainment. They are problem-aware to solution-aware. Pins should promise an idea or a solution, and the landing page must deliver exactly what the pin showed.

# Part 10 — Email

- The list is the asset we own. Every other channel is rented (Kennedy, Halbert, M).
- **Halbert's A-pile:** people sort mail into personal and junk, and open the personal first. Write and format emails so they feel like one person writing to one person.
- **Welcome sequence:** the highest-earning emails a brand sends. Deliver the promised thing, tell the story, state what we stand for, show proof, make the first offer.
- **Abandoned cart:** a series, not one email. The best-performing subject lines simply remind them they left something. Short. Key message first, because phones cut subject lines off. A large button straight back to the cart with the item shown.
- **Subject lines:** specific, short, written like a person. The preview text is a second headline.
- **Every email:** one idea, one action.
- **Reciprocity:** most emails should give something — an idea, a tip, a story — before asking.
- Frequency follows value. People unsubscribe from boring, not from frequent (M).

# Part 11 — Selling a service

- **Sheridan's Big Five.** Buyers research five things before they call: cost, problems, comparisons, reviews, and best-in-class. Answer all five on the site, honestly, including price and including our weaknesses. When a buyer cannot find the price they assume we are hiding it.
- **Enns.** Specialise. The narrow expert does not pitch and does not discount; the generalist does both. Expertise is the differentiator, not personality or price. Diagnose before prescribing.
- **Abraham.** Act as the trusted adviser. Tell them when they do not need us.
- **Make it tangible.** Named people, a described process with timings, photographs of the work, case results with numbers, a guarantee, a low-risk first step.
- **Price signals quality** when there is little else to judge by. Cheap reads as risky.
- **Say who it is not for.** It raises trust with the people it is for.
- **Speed of reply** is part of the product. The first to respond usually wins (M).

# Part 12 — Price and premium

- Never apologise for price. Justify it with what it does, how it is made and what it replaces.
- Anchor against the true alternative, not a made-up "was" price.
- Reframe the unit: per use, per day, per year of life (Sutherland).
- Discounting trains buyers to wait. Prefer added value: a bonus, a bundle, an upgrade.
- If discounting, give the reason and the end date.
- Premium cues: space, restraint, few words, excellent photography, one typeface family, slow pace, consistent detail. A crowded page reads as cheap regardless of price.
- Effort and cost that are visible signal confidence: heavy packaging, a handwritten note, a long guarantee (Sutherland).
- Names matter. The same thing under a better name is a better thing (Sutherland).

# Part 13 — Growth mechanics

- **Hormozi's core four.** There are only four ways to get leads: reach out to people who know us, publish content, reach out to strangers, run paid ads. Pick deliberately.
- **Abraham's three levers.** Customers × order value × frequency.
- **Brunson's diagnostic.** When a funnel fails, check hook, then story, then offer.
- **Two jobs, two kinds of creative.** Brand work (emotional, broad, slow) and activation (rational, targeted, fast). Do both; do not blend them in one piece.
- **Follow-up.** Most sales come after the first contact. Build the sequence before buying the traffic (Kennedy, M).

---

# Part 14 — Testing discipline

- Hopkins and Caples built their rules by counting coupons. The method matters more than any rule it produced.
- Test in this order: market, offer, headline or hook, proof, page structure, then small details. Big levers first.
- One change at a time. Enough traffic to mean something.
- Laja: other people's test results answer other people's problems. Ours are specific. Research first — recordings, surveys, reviews — then test.
- Keep a log of every test, result and conclusion per brand.
- Reality checks from the research literature: subtle "priming" tricks do not survive replication; charm pricing has a small effect; too many choices hurts mainly when options are hard to compare. Do not build on tricks. Build on clarity, proof and offer.

# Part 15 — The audit checklist

Score 0 (absent), 1 (partial), 2 (strong).

**Market**
1. The target customer is defined by a belief or situation, not a demographic.
2. A voice-of-customer file exists and copy visibly uses its language.
3. Each main landing page matches the awareness rung of its traffic.

**Positioning and brand**
4. One-sentence positioning exists and has no "and".
5. A stranger can say what this is, who for and why different in five seconds.
6. Distinctive assets are identical across site, email, pins, video, packaging.
7. The About page states beliefs and who it is for.

**Offer**
8. Outcome, believability, speed and ease are each stated.
9. Price is anchored.
10. A named guarantee sits beside the buy button.
11. There is a true reason to act now.
12. There is a free or low-cost first step.

**Copy**
13. Headlines pass picture / falsify / only-us.
14. First line is about the reader.
15. Benefits before features; reasons given.
16. Captions sell.
17. No sentence a competitor could use unchanged.

**Proof**
18. Proof appears in the first screen.
19. Every product has five or more reviews, negatives visible.
20. Proof sits beside the objection it answers.
21. Real people and real photographs.

**Page**
22. One primary action per page, repeated.
23. First screen answers what, for whom, what next.
24. Full cost visible before checkout.
25. Guest checkout, minimal fields, fast on a phone.

**Video and social**
26. Hook lands inside two seconds, in picture and text.
27. Each video rewards the hook.
28. Captions on; native look; one idea.
29. Retention at three seconds and completion are tracked.

**Pinterest**
30. 2:3, in-context image, short overlay, subtle logo, keyworded.
31. Landing page matches the pin exactly.

**Email**
32. Welcome sequence and cart series exist.
33. Emails read as one person to one person; one action each.

**Service (where relevant)**
34. Price or range is published.
35. Process, people and results are shown.
36. The Big Five questions are answered on the site.

**Discipline**
37. A test log exists.
38. Nothing on the site is untrue.
